The rapid development of LLMs allows us to hand over more and more work to machines. This inevitably raises a question: when abilities that once took years of study and training to acquire become readily available, where should we go from here?

Let us first consider the differences between humans and LLMs. Both can reason, yet their underlying structures are very different.

Throughout the long history of biological evolution, nervous systems have faced a direct demand: to sense their surroundings and respond more quickly and effectively. Thicker axons and the emergence of myelin are both evolutionary solutions for speeding up electrical signal transmission. Individuals that could escape danger, obtain food, and coordinate their bodies in time generally had a better chance of survival. Extended symbolic reasoning, however, fits less naturally into this evolutionary history—or rather, it was not what natural selection directly optimized for.

How, then, does a nervous system that has long served perception and response come to carry out such reasoning? I like to think of thought as a form of association: activity in one group of neurons brings about activity in another. Associations form the basic units of thought, while memory, rules, and feedback allow some of them to develop into reusable paths of reasoning. To keep following these paths, however, we need to remember intermediate results and maintain the relationships between steps.

Language, writing, and other symbolic tools provide a kind of external storage. We can record the steps from p to q and from q to r, and pass the resulting connection between p and r down as a conclusion that others can use directly. The development of thought thus gained a driving force beyond natural selection: human communities began deliberately cultivating the thinking abilities of individuals, developing ideas, theories, and systems of training. The systematic ways of reasoning we use today are, to a large extent, the result of learning. With these tools, an ancient nervous system can take on increasingly complex tasks.

LLMs take a different route. For autoregressive language models, basic pretraining is organized directly around next-token prediction: predicting the next token from the preceding context and the model’s parameters. This is a form of conditional generation. Further post-training can improve the model’s ability to carry out multistep reasoning and complete tasks.

To cultivate thinking, human education usually begins with relatively fixed mappings: recognizing symbols, remembering definitions, and mastering methods, followed by various forms of practice. Yet if training largely stops at producing expected outputs from given inputs, much of what it develops is an ability for conditional generation. How are we to compete on that basis with LLMs that are optimized around this objective from the very beginning of their training?

Modern education develops our problem-solving abilities, but its selection mechanisms can also invite a kind of reward hacking: scores are meant to measure understanding and thought, yet students may achieve impressive results by becoming familiar with question types and applying well-practiced methods. In the LLM era, we all seem to have become, in a broad sense, expert test-takers, while some of the abilities we have spent so long developing are becoming increasingly easy for LLMs to replace. When problem-solving is no longer scarce, what is left for us to do?

My answer is inspiration.

Inspiration is an elusive thing. I suspect that, when we think, we maintain a kind of “memory cache” related to the task at hand. The knowledge within it is easier to retrieve or associate with, forming the basis for our own conditional generation. Inspiration may arise from neural activity outside this “cache”: memories or concepts that were not part of our current train of thought happen to surface and connect with the problem we are working on. In my experience, this happens more readily when I am relatively relaxed. When deeply focused, my thinking is more active and my reasoning faster, but I can also become confined to familiar patterns of association. Going for a walk or playing a game can, instead, occasionally bring an unexpected idea.

To me, inspiration—the ability to bring “something out of nothing”—means stepping outside the original framing of a problem, asking new questions, or finding unexpected approaches. This is one aspect that I currently find harder for LLMs to replace. As more of the work of execution and verification can be handed over to LLMs, discovering what is worth trying and deciding which direction to explore will become more important parts of our work.
