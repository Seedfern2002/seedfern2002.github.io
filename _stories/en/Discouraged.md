Recently, in a short interview related to the Fields Medal, Peking University alumnus Wang Hong described his undergraduate experience as **“discouraged.”** I found that word deeply relatable, so I wanted to share a few thoughts of my own.

Many people think that students feel discouraged at elite universities because the transition from high school to university brings a dramatic change in relative ranking and, with it, a strong sense of disappointment. I do not think that is really the case. Most students, at least among those I knew, did not think they were doing exceptionally well before university in the first place. Perhaps this has something to do with the traditional Chinese emphasis on modesty and keeping a low profile. To me, the deeper reason behind this feeling of being **discouraged** lies in the way the educational system is structured. I want to discuss this from two perspectives: **a narrow evaluation system**, and **a lack of encouragement for individuality and open-ended exploration**. I will focus more on the latter, drawing mainly from my own experience.

## A Narrow Evaluation System

Before university, given China’s enormous population, the Gaokao—a standardised and largely non-specialised examination—serves as a relatively fair and efficient way to select students. Gaokao scores are certainly a strong indicator of academic ability, but students selected through such a system are, to some extent, inevitably fitted to exam-taking and tend to care a great deal about grades. This habit of mind usually persists for some time after they enter university.

At university, schools do encourage students to develop in multiple directions. Students are encouraged to participate in research, societies, student organisations, and other activities, and scholarship evaluations also take such experiences into account to some extent. But when it comes to outcomes such as graduate applications or guaranteed postgraduate admission, unless you already have sufficiently strong publications or connections, GPA remains the most important indicator. Other experiences often matter far less.

Compared with other forms of achievement, GPA is an extremely stable indicator. If your goal is to maximise your expected personal return, then exam-taking—a skill that students have already spent years perfecting—makes improving GPA a relatively low-cost choice. Even when there are quotas on top grades, improving one’s GPA is still the optimal strategy for many students, because other paths are full of uncertainty and their potential rewards often take a long time to materialise. On top of that, many students are risk-averse and follow the logic that “having a high GPA can never be a bad thing.” Naturally, they become more willing to invest time in improving their grades, which further intensifies the competition.

## A Lack of Encouragement for Individuality and Open-Ended Exploration

“Universities only select students; they do not cultivate them.” This is how many people describe undergraduate education in China, and I think there is a lot of truth to it. Chinese universities have abundant resources, but students are often expected to discover and fight for those resources on their own. Universities generally hope that students will follow the prescribed curriculum step by step, gain some research experience, and proceed along an established path. They also provide some degree of safety net for students who do so—for example, through guaranteed admission to postgraduate programmes. But allowing students to grow only along predefined routes gradually damages their possibilities. For students themselves, the opportunity cost and risk involved in exploring other directions are simply too high, and there are very few departments or institutional mechanisms willing to provide a safety net for those who choose to explore. Of course, there are always extraordinarily talented students who can have everything at once—effortlessly getting an extremely high GPA while still having plenty of time to pursue whatever interests them. But for most students, university becomes a game of trade-offs: either exchange possibility for a future that has already been planned out, or give up a stable return in order to preserve your possibilities.

I loved mathematics when I was a child, but through a series of coincidences, I ended up doing biology competitions in high school. During my second year of high school, I spent two months as an exchange student at what was probably the most toxic high school in China, and the experience had an enormous negative impact on my mental state. Later, I got food poisoning during the national biology competition and performed disastrously. After all of this, I developed something close to severe PTSD around biology. At around the same time, the Strong Foundation Programme was introduced. I lost the direct admissions advantage that competition students had previously enjoyed, and my raw Gaokao score was also below Peking University’s regular admission threshold, so I could only enter PKU through the Strong Foundation Programme. Once I was admitted, I was not allowed to change my major—at least that was the policy for the programme’s first cohort. At that point, I already had a strong feeling that my undergraduate life was going to be extremely painful.

After we enrolled, the department advised us to take more courses, finish the required curriculum as early as possible, not worry too much about GPA, enter a laboratory early, and then continue to graduate school through guaranteed postgraduate admission. This was exactly the kind of stable, carefully planned path I described above. But I knew very clearly that, because of what had happened during my biology competition years, I simply could not continue down that path. While feeling lost, I spent a great deal of time on my hobbies: the violin society, the departmental football team, student organisations, and so on. Among all of these, the one that had the greatest impact on me was programming. When I first encountered programming, I became fascinated by this computational system that had been artificially designed by humans. In my introductory computing course, the final project was to implement an agent for a board game, and I found it incredibly interesting. Although the project counted for only a tiny fraction of the final grade, I spent an enormous amount of time designing strategies and testing them over and over again. After the project ended, I learned a little about reinforcement learning, and that opened a new door for me. Of course, the price was substantial: in my first semester, I took 29 credits across 13 courses and ended up with an extremely low GPA. This almost gave me what I jokingly thought of as **GPA freedom**—there was so little chance of fully recovering it later that I no longer had much to lose.

During the winter break, Professor Rao Yi posted two books in our course group and wrote, “If these two little books cannot spark your interest, leave biology early.” Forget being interested—I felt distressed just looking at them. That made me realise even more clearly that I had to find another path. I also realised that I was in very poor physical and psychological condition at the time, so I took fewer courses in the second semester to recover. That semester, I applied for a double major in computer science and also learned more about applying for master’s programmes abroad. I set myself the goal of raising my GPA to 3.75 before applications, hoping that applying for a CS master’s could serve as a fallback option. During the summer after my first year, following my interest in reinforcement learning, I learned some deep learning and even rented a server to participate in a small competition.

From my second to third year, I gradually found my comfort zone for improving my GPA: mathematics courses offered by the School of Electronics Engineering and Computer Science, as well as project-based courses. I had studied a little mathematics competition material in high school, and while preparing for biology competitions I had also learned some statistics. As a result, I did reasonably well in courses such as algorithms, discrete mathematics, probability, and stochastic processes. At the same time, I needed to improve my programming skills, so all kinds of course projects became excellent opportunities to practise. During this period, I also met many professors and friends who helped me enormously, and I made several attempts at research, but I still could not find a direction that genuinely interested me.

Then I entered my fourth year. An unexpected reform of the grading system dealt me a heavy blow. In order to “reduce students’ burden,” the School of Life Sciences decided to replace the four-point GPA system with a letter-grade system, and the change took effect immediately even for fourth-year students. This meant that when applying abroad, I could no longer obtain an official four-point GPA or class ranking. PKU’s original transcript reports scores on a 100-point scale, which had previously been converted into a four-point GPA using a quadratic formula, but graduate applications often require applicants to enter a GPA on a four-point scale. I became deeply anxious about how much uncertainty this would introduce into my applications abroad, and I applied to an internal PKU programme with a relatively low admissions threshold, hoping to switch fields through guaranteed postgraduate admission. I did not even get an interview.

That evening, after going to the programme’s administrative office and arguing my case without success, I thought for a very long time. I did not understand why there were so many random policy changes. I did not understand why every institutional advantage seemed to favour students who had followed the expected trajectory. I did not understand what I was supposed to do to secure a stable ending for myself. As the sky began to brighten, I suddenly felt that I had been rather foolish. I had always believed that randomness and possibility were among the most valuable things in life, and yet I was desperately trying to find a certain ending. At that moment, I made up my mind: I would finish my applications to CS programmes abroad. A few hours later, the administrative office of that internal programme called me. Someone had failed to show up for an interview, and they now had an extra interview slot for me. I turned it down without hesitation.

In the end, my application results were brutal. Some universities rejected me directly because of my primary major, something I learned by comparing application information and outcomes with friends from PKU’s computer science programmes. This happened even though I had a double major in computer science. In fact, if you removed the biology-related courses from my transcript, the remaining courses would have been more than enough for me to graduate in computer science—and my GPA would have been higher as well. Fortunately, I eventually received an offer from the MSc in Computer Science programme at EPFL, and my experience here has made me feel as though I have been given a new life.

In today’s standardised educational system, preserving possibility is extremely difficult. And I believe that a good education system should allow those who choose to explore not to pay such an overwhelming price simply for keeping their possibilities open.
